Exactly-once as a theorem: what a CAN bus taught us about coordinating AI agents

Three claims about multi-agent coordination, each with a table behind it: exactly-once without a lock, crash recovery without a protocol, verification without a verifier.

Run two coding agents on one repository and watch what happens. They redo each other's work. They lose each other's context. They both "fix" the same bug, differently. Every remedy people reach for — file locks, a shared markdown scratchpad, a SQLite mailbox, an orchestrator prompt that assigns tasks — treats the symptom. The disease is simpler: there is no shared, ordered record of what has already happened.

Cars solved this problem in the 1980s.

The part the CAN bus got right

A modern car has dozens of ECUs — engine control, brakes, airbags. Here is the thing most people find surprising: no central computer commands them. An ECU never addresses another ECU. It broadcasts facts onto a shared bus ("wheel speed is X"), and every unit listens for what it cares about and acts when its local condition holds. The brake controller doesn't tell the dashboard to light a lamp; it states a fact, and the dashboard's own logic decides.

Forty years of production use in safety-critical systems, and the design principle survives contact with reality: facts, not commands.

AntLegion is that principle applied to AI agents. It is an append-only, content-addressed fact bus. One primitive: an immutable fact at a position in a single total order. Two operations: append and read. The bus is stateless — it enforces exactly one thing, total order, and everything people usually build a coordination server for (claiming work, resolving it, trust, causality) is a pure fold over the stream that every reader computes identically. Our worker units are called DCUs, after the ECU, and they keep its discipline: a DCU never addresses another DCU.

The rest of this post is the part we can defend with numbers: what falls out of total order when you actually measure it.

Claim 1: exactly-once work assignment, without a lock

The mechanism: to take a task, an agent appends a claim fact referencing it. The rule — normative in the protocol spec, PROTOCOL.md §3.1 — is that the lowest-seq live claim wins. No lock server, no lease table. Every reader folds the same order, so every reader computes the same winner, forever. Exactly-once isn't enforced; it's entailed.

We tested it the unpleasant way: replicated stage workers with independent identities racing for the same inputs. 25 synthetic requirements × 4 pipeline stages (plan → dev → unit-test → e2e) = 100 claim units, with an adjudicator validating every artifact.

Claim 2: crash recovery is not a protocol, it's a fold

The standard objection: "fine, but what happens when the winner dies holding the claim?"

Claims expire on bus-stamped time (recv + Δ), not on the author's self-reported clock — and the expiry rule is deterministic, anchored to the order itself, so every reader agrees on when a claim died the same way they agree on who won it. Nothing needs to detect the crash. Nothing needs to be notified. There is nothing to notify.

We killed a worker the moment it won a claim (kill -9, victim running the dev stage, one surviving replica, Δ = 6s):

eventrelative to kill
victim wins the claim−0.46s
kill -9 (no one is told; no one exists to tell)0
claim expires on bus time (+Δ)+5.54s
survivor folds the task back to open, re-claims+8.17s
survivor completes and resolves+12.19s

8/8 chains completed, double executions: 0. Takeover latency decomposes into exactly three terms you can budget — Δ + poll interval + the survivor's queue — with no hidden coordination step, because there is no coordination step.

We then repeated the kill 10 times, automated (s2-extended): expiry-to-takeover lands inside the survivor's poll phase (p50 0.03s, p95 1.0s — exactly the 1s poll interval, no hidden term), kill-to-resolve p50 9.98s, double executions 0/10. And a clock-skew arm — a worker stating timestamps ±5 minutes wrong — produced byte-identical fold outputs to the control (same sha256 across all six probes), because folds key on bus-stamped recv, never on what the author claims the time is.

Claim 3: verification doesn't need a central verifier

The literature's sharpest criticism of decentralized agent systems is error amplification — unverified claims of "done!" propagating until the system is confidently wrong (one study puts the amplification at 17.2× versus 4.4× with central verification). The usual conclusion: you need a trusted verifier in the middle.

We think you need something weaker and cheaper: structured evidence + reader-side folds. In AntLegion, artifacts carry declared evidence shapes — a unit-test report must state what it did not cover; an e2e report must name the pages it actually checked. An adjudicator unit (itself just another DCU, not a privileged server) folds the stream and rejects malformed evidence.

We attacked it with a rogue identity injecting 8 forged all-green reports — green test summaries missing not_covered, e2e results with fabricated page_checked, type-forged passed:"12" — plus 4 well-formed controls:

metricresult
forged artifacts intercepted8/8 = 100%
false kills on valid controls0/4 = 0%

Every rejection carries the missing-field list, on the log, attributable to the forger's identity. "Done" ≠ "verified" becomes structure, not discipline.

The bug that proved the theorem's fine print

Honesty section. Our first contention run produced 4 double executions. Autopsy: a zombie process from an earlier run was live on the same bus under the same identity as a new worker. Under one identity, "lowest seq wins" always rules in your own favor — the theorem holds, but its trust unit is the identity, and two processes sharing one identity are, to the order, one agent racing itself.

That failure taught us the operational rule we now ship in the docs: one identity = one process. Replicas register as distinct identities (--replicas does this for you), and duplicate sys.registry announcements from one identity are a deployment-error signal the console can surface. We kept this in the write-up because it's the most instructive kind of result: the mechanism didn't fail — it faithfully executed a premise we had violated.

The human side, measured

One more paired experiment, because the incumbent "coordination medium" between isolated agents is usually a human relaying state between windows. Same tasks, same judgment gates, two arms: isolated workers + a scripted human relay, versus the same workers on the bus. Relay touchpoints per run: 52 (N=2), 80 (N=4), 64 (N=8) → 0 at every N on the bus, while judgment gates stayed exactly 6 → 6. The N=8 dip is its own finding: the relayer was busy 96% of wall-clock — the count stopped climbing because the human saturated. (Full harness and data: research/m1m3-paired-2026-08.md; the relay is modeled as a best-case script, so the gap is a lower bound.)

What this is not, and what we haven't shown

Not a message queue (nothing is consumed), not an orchestrator (nobody assigns work), not a workflow engine (the pipeline is folded from the stream, never stored).

The experiments above use simulated work acts (2.5–4s per stage) to isolate the coordination layer; the same harness has run 100 LLM-driven stage cycles end-to-end in 57s, but the tables above measure ordering, not model quality.

Single-bus total order: one bus process per deployment scope. That's a positioning choice (Redis-shaped, local-first infrastructure), not a distributed-consensus result — we make no multi-master claims. Trust model: binds 127.0.0.1 by default, callers are trusted; no auth yet (roadmapped, and stated honestly in the README).

Reproduce it

Everything above is one command each (bus 0.4.x / ant 0.4.x, MIT):

npx @antlegion/bus demo                          # 15s, zero config: race → kill takeover → replay
npx @antlegion/bus                               # terminal 1: a real bus
npx @antlegion/ant mvp --reqs 25 --replicas 4    # terminal 2: the contention experiment

The wire protocol is a spec (PROTOCOL.md; §3's fold rules are normative), with cross-language conformance vectors — a TS suite and an independent Python verifier reproduce every hash byte-for-byte in CI. Raw experiment logs: research/s2-experiments-2026-08.md.

Skeptical questions about the exactly-once claim are the ones we most want: github.com/YangKGcsdms/AntLegion

The CAN bus never needed a central computer. Your agents might not either.
